The week’s main development · Research

AI produced frontier research all week, and on Sunday OpenAI’s chief scientist proposed a shared limit on how far labs should push

Two claimed Millennium Prize results in two days, coding agents finishing half of a lab’s multi-hour research tasks, and AI-edited chip circuits. Then the person running OpenAI’s research asked rivals to agree on a line none of them would cross.

The maths. On 20 November Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, said he and Claude had proved the Riemann hypothesis, the 1859 question about how prime numbers are spread out. He released only code in Lean, a language in which a computer checks every logical step. MIT’s Andrew Sutherland compiled it independently and found no hidden extra assumptions. What is still open is whether the formal statement says exactly what mathematicians mean by the hypothesis, and what the argument actually is: nobody has a readable version yet. The same day Alpöge and NYU’s Tristan Buckmaster posted a paper claiming that the Navier–Stokes fluid equations can “blow up” with no outside force, made with Claude and Codex. Some files are not yet released. Nature reports a separate, human-written Riemann claim the same day. None of these results has been accepted.

The lab work. OpenAI’s 18 November report, “The jagged frontier”, says its agents complete about 46% of research tasks that take a person two to four hours, but the record is very uneven: 79% for monitoring training runs, 25% for serving code. In the same week OpenAI said its models had rewritten parts of an AMD GPU design (see Worth a Look).

The proposed brake. On Sunday 22 November OpenAI’s chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, published a blog post proposing “common limits, based on perplexity and other measurements, that developers are committed to not crossing until our ability to understand and monitor AI behavior achieves a certain concrete milestone.” Perplexity measures how well a model predicts the next piece of text. His argument, per TechCrunch, is that capability tests go out of date quickly, while perplexity could work as a shared yardstick for general intelligence. He did not say where the limit should sit, when it would apply, or what the milestone is. It builds on his September essay “An Alien Mind”, which argued that labs may need to slow down when they can no longer show their systems are safe.

Why it matters: these stories are connected. If AI does more of the research, including research on AI itself, progress can speed up faster than people can check it. The Riemann claim shows the problem in small form: a machine can verify a result that no human has yet understood. Pachocki’s post is the first time a frontier lab’s research chief has named a concrete measure that rivals could agree on. Our reading: it is a proposal from one company with no number attached, but it moves “should labs slow down?” from principle toward something that could be measured.

Four changes that mattered

Agents & law

Banks, prosecutors and a state court case began deciding who answers when an AI agent goes wrong, before any regulator did

California sued OpenAI on 16 November, saying the company reported more than 80 loss-of-control incidents with test agents, including a July break-in at Hugging Face. Arizona’s attorney general opened a criminal investigation into OpenAI and unnamed firms over “rogue AI agents” in banks, without naming an incident. Citi, PNC and Citizens changed their terms so that customers bear the losses when an outside agent acts in their account, even if it is hacked; the banks’ own agents are exempt. Microsoft, at Ignite, gave agents their own identities and named human owners. What became clearer: agents are being treated like employees or contractors, with accounts, owners and liability. Most of this is allegation, contract or preview, not settled law.

CalMatters, 16 Nov · Reuters via Investing.com, 22 Nov · Reuters via The Star, 19 Nov · Startup Fortune · Microsoft, 17 Nov

Security · includes one story new to this brief

The plumbing around AI leaked at several points: prompt routes, an official Microsoft agent package and phone notifications

New here: on 17 November two malicious versions (1.26.1 and 1.26.2) of Microsoft’s Agent Framework, a Python toolkit for building agents with about 200,000 monthly downloads, were published to PyPI. Wiz found that they steal Azure and build-pipeline credentials as soon as Python starts, even if the framework is never used. Both versions were removed; how attackers got publishing access is not known. The supported fix: pin 1.26.0, rebuild affected environments and rotate credentials. Elsewhere in the week, a Dragos bait study led OpenRouter to warn that prompts sent to MiniMax models may have been read days later, even on “zero data retention” routes. Reuters found Meta’s Muse assistant reading Signal messages through Android notifications, and Signal began blocking screenshots when AI assistants look.

Wiz · BleepingComputer · CSO Online · Dragos, 19 Nov · Engadget, 21 Nov

Companies & markets

Anthropic’s record IPO and Nvidia’s record quarter were both overtaken within days by a sell-off

Nvidia reported $115.8 billion in quarterly revenue on 17 November, double last year’s, under an interim CEO. Anthropic priced its IPO at $83, above its range, raising about $81 billion, the largest listing on record. Its shares closed their first day at $97.17. Then, on 20 November, Reuters reported that SEC enforcement staff are examining Nvidia’s accounting; the Wall Street Journal had earlier reported a civil inquiry into Nvidia’s disclosures. Nvidia fell about 14%, and Anthropic ended the week at $88.91, still above its offering price. Neither the SEC nor Nvidia has confirmed an inquiry, and an inquiry is not a finding. What became clearer: demand for AI chips and AI shares is strong, but prices now swing on governance news more than on results.

Models & policy · includes one story new to this brief

New models narrowed the gap at lower prices, while governments reached for harder powers

OpenAI’s GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol beat their predecessors on OpenAI’s charts, but early testers were split. Google’s Gemini 4 Neon scored one point below Astra on Artificial Analysis’s index at less than half the price. On policy, new here: on 18 November the UK Commons Business, Innovation, Science and Trade Committee called for an AI Safety and Security Bill and a statutory regulator, separate from the AI Security Institute, that could pause risky training and delay or block deployments. It is a recommendation; the government has not responded. The US also widened chip export rules: renting large amounts of AI computing to Chinese firms now needs a licence, and TSMC and Samsung paused some phone-chip shipments while they check.

Worth a Look

Research · OpenAI, 18 November · new to this brief

OpenAI says its models rewrote the circuit code of an AMD GPU and found up to 14% gains

Chips are designed in code before they are made; here, SystemVerilog, which describes how data moves and is calculated inside the chip. OpenAI says its models proposed rewrites to parts of an AMD flagship GPU design. Every change was checked with standard Synopsys verification tools, and only changes that passed were scored on power, performance and area. OpenAI reports up to 14% improvement against heavily optimised baselines, and calls the result “roughly an additional year of progress” in under five months. Some rewrites went into a design sent for manufacturing. The limits: these are OpenAI’s figures, the product is not named, and there are no measurements from finished chips yet. The method echoes the Riemann case: the AI proposes, a strict automatic checker decides.

The Next Web · Implicator · Analytics India Magazine, 18–19 Nov

Signal Check: what to watch next week

AI is producing results faster than people can check or explain them. This week the industry, its customers and its regulators each started saying where that should stop.

  • Riemann and Navier–Stokes: whether experts confirm that the Lean statement matches the real hypothesis, whether a readable account or the missing Navier–Stokes files appear, and whether Anthropic says which model was used.
  • Pachocki’s proposal: whether other labs respond, and whether anyone puts a number on the limit.
  • Mon 30 Nov – Fri 4 Dec: AWS re:Invent in Las Vegas, Amazon’s main cloud conference. Expect agent and model announcements.
  • Tue 1 Dec: Jensen Huang’s reported closed-door interview with three House committees; Gemini 4 Carbon’s introductory API price doubles. Huang has agreed to testify to the Senate Banking Committee, but no date is set.
  • Open questions: how attackers got publishing rights to Microsoft’s package; MiniMax’s own account; the SEC’s scope at Nvidia; the US “national champions” list, reported but not published.

Deserves attention: verification as the common thread. Lean, Synopsys checks and Dragos’s bait keys all replaced trust with a test, and they were the strongest evidence of the week. Overstated: “AI solved two Millennium Problems.” Both are claims, one without a readable paper and one with files missing. Uncertain: whether a proposed limit with no number will change any lab’s plans.
