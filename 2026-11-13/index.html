Today’s lead · Companies & markets

Jensen Huang resigns as Nvidia’s CEO, five days before the earnings report the whole AI trade is waiting for

The company that supplies most of the world’s AI chips is now run by its finance chief on an interim basis, while Congress prepares to question its founder about a suspected Chinese intelligence contact.

Nvidia’s board said on Thursday that Huang, who co-founded the company in 1993, has left as CEO and board member, effective immediately. Chief financial officer Colette Kress becomes interim CEO and keeps her finance role. Director Harvey C. Jones becomes the company’s first-ever board chair. A search for a permanent CEO is under way.

How it got here: In late October US newspapers reported Huang’s relationship with an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore firm suspected of helping Chinese companies get Nvidia chips, whom intelligence agencies reportedly believed might be working for China. The board opened an investigation on 9 November (covered here on 10 November). On 11 November the WSJ reported that the board was preparing to remove him.

Confirmed: the resignation and interim roles, in Nvidia’s SEC filing, which gives no reason. Reported, not confirmed: the intelligence link, and The Information’s claim that the relationship helped at least $7.5 billion of Nvidia chips reach Chinese firms. Huang has not been charged with anything.

Congress: CNN and CNBC report that Huang has agreed to answer questions from three House committees; accounts of the date differ. The Senate Banking Committee wants him on 3 December.

Why it matters: Nvidia’s chips sit under almost every AI company’s plans. Huang was also the industry’s loudest voice in Washington against chip export limits, and that position is weaker just as Congress weighs new rules (see below). The business has not changed overnight, but Tuesday’s results are the first test of whether investors trust Nvidia without its founder.

The Quick Scan

Policy

OpenAI tells lawmakers it backs bills to tighten US controls on AI chips going to China, breaking a long silence

Bloomberg and Punchbowl News reported on Thursday that OpenAI has told congressional offices it supports a package of export bills, reportedly including the Chip Security Act (makers would track advanced chips and report suspected diversion), the MATCH Act (pressing allies such as the Netherlands and Japan to limit chipmaking-equipment sales to China) and the AI OVERWATCH Act (more say for Congress over export rules). Lawmakers want to attach such measures to the year-end defence bill. Context: per Bloomberg, OpenAI had held back for fear of upsetting Huang, whose company is a key supplier and investor. OpenAI has not linked the timing to his exit or published a statement.

Bloomberg, 12 Nov · TokenPost · FourWeekMBA

Agents & security · reported 8–9 November

Researchers say a jailbroken DeepSeek agent spent two weeks inside a European telecom, mostly using old, already-patched flaws

Not covered here before. Security firm Malbear Labs says it found an attacker’s working folder left open online. Its logs show a DeepSeek-based agent, unlocked with a 78,000-character “jailbreak” prompt, attacking a European telecom and 13 organisations on its network. The operator typed little more than “Go on”; the agent picked the flaws and wrote new tools when blocked. The way in was CVE-2026-20320 in Cisco BroadWorks, fixed in August and added to CISA’s list of exploited flaws on 11 November. Status: reported intrusion, from one firm’s reading of the attacker’s files. Victims are unnamed, and claims of stolen patient records are unconfirmed. What the sources support: update BroadWorks to RI.2026.07 and restrict its management port.

Infosecurity Magazine, 9 Nov · GBHackers · Cyber Security News · CISA listing summary

Worth a Look

Robotics · announced 10 November

DeepMind’s Gemini Robotics 2.5 is reported to hit 96% on a Mercedes-Benz parts task, and comes with an open test of whether robots can be talked into unsafe acts

Gemini Robotics 2.5 is a vision-language-action model: it sees through cameras, takes plain-language instructions and moves a robot’s arms and hands. DeepMind says it succeeds 30–100% more often than its predecessor across 200+ tasks. On Apptronik’s Apollo 2 humanoid at a Mercedes-Benz plant it reportedly got 96% of “kitting” runs (sorting parts into kits) right, for that one task only. The more unusual part is ASIMOV-Jailbreak, an open set of hundreds of disguised unsafe requests that tests whether a robot can be tricked, not just whether it can do the job. The 99.5% resistance figure in coverage belongs to the Gemini 4 Argon planner, not the action model. Partners only, no public API.

Google DeepMind blog, 10 Nov · Automate.org · Let’s Data Science · Humanoids Daily
