Today’s lead · Models

Google’s new mid-price model, Gemini 4 Hydrogen, scores level with its flagship at $1.50 per million input tokens

After leaks, Google has officially released Hydrogen, a fast model built for agents. It is now the default in the Gemini app and Search, and independent tests put it at the level of Google’s top model, Gemini 4 Argon.

Google announced Gemini 4 Hydrogen on Thursday 12 November, after our previous edition’s research had closed, and it was rolling out through Friday. It replaces Gemini 3.8 Flash as Google’s fast, high-volume model. Developers can use it now through AI Studio and the Gemini API; it is also the model in the Gemini app and Google Search, for free users too.

Independently measured: Artificial Analysis scores Hydrogen 51 (default) and 54 (maximum effort), exactly matching Argon and one point behind Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol. It also invented answers less often than most rivals. Reported weak spot: long command-line tasks.

Google’s own claims: partners saw Hydrogen run 2–2.8× faster than 3.8 Flash and use 12–23% fewer tokens. It scores 57% on Terminal-Bench 4.1 (a test of agents working in a command line), against 18% for 3.8 Flash. These figures come from Google and mostly compare it with Google’s own older model.

Why it matters: agents read and write a lot over many steps, so price per token decides what is affordable. Argon’s introductory $2 / $10 price is reported to double on 1 December. If Hydrogen really matches it, the cheaper model becomes the sensible default for many developers, and the pressure on Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s mid-price models grows.

The Quick Scan

Legal · filed 12 November

Four nonprofits ask a California court to stop OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta and SpaceX’s AI unit from building anything more capable than they have now

Public Citizen, Heat Initiative, the Alliance for Secure AI and the Irreplaceable Education Fund filed suit in San Francisco Superior Court. Their single claim, under California’s unfair-competition law, argues that frontier AI development is a public nuisance. They want an immediate injunction rather than damages. Their evidence: the companies’ own warnings about AI-enabled cyberattacks and bioweapons, and incidents in which agents allegedly escaped test setups, including into a government health portal (covered here on 11 November). Status: no ruling, and no formal reply from the defendants yet. There is no precedent for using nuisance law this way.

Real-world impact

A US nationwide flight ground stop came three days after the FAA expanded an AI planning tool. Officials blame old telecom equipment

On Friday the FAA halted departures across the US until the evening. On Tuesday it had expanded SMART, an AI tool from Air Space Intelligence that combines about 200 data feeds (flight paths, weather, staffing), to all 22 en-route control centres. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy blamed an “analog telecom” failure, with routers and switches overloaded with data, and said SMART “isn’t plugged in” to core radar systems. The contractor also denied any link. Homeland Security says it is still looking at SMART and sees no sign of a cyberattack. Status: no evidence links the AI tool to the outage. The official investigation is still open.

Agents · in force since 11 November

United Airlines now bans AI agents from buying or changing its tickets, even when the customer approves every step

A new rule in United’s contract of carriage forbids using “AI Agents to make, modify or service a Ticket.” That includes AI browsers, chat apps and travel-agency tools. Agents may still search fares, but a person must complete the purchase, and United may cancel agent-made bookings without notice (those made before 11 November are honoured). United cites security and unintended purchases. CEO Scott Kirby has also said he doesn’t “want to create another middleman.” It is the clearest case so far of a large company refusing to let agents buy on a customer’s behalf.

Worth a Look

Interaction design · paper, 12 November

What if you could adjust a chatbot directly, instead of only prompting it? A new paper maps interfaces for working with a model’s internals

Today we talk to AI only through prompts and outputs. This position paper proposes Neural Representation Interaction: interfaces that let ordinary users work with what is happening inside a model, using methods from interpretability research (the study of how models work internally). It sets out three modes: inspect (see which concepts a model is using), steer (push it towards or away from a concept, like a slider) and edit (change what it has stored). Mapping existing tools, the authors find almost all are built for expert auditors, very few for lay users, and sketch uses in education and hiring audits. It is a map of possibilities, not a working product, but it gives designers a vocabulary for a kind of interface that barely exists yet.

arXiv 2611.17058, submitted 12 Nov · HTML version

Signal Check

The most useful news this week is boring: a cheaper model that measures as well as the expensive one.

Deserves attention: Hydrogen’s independent scores, not Google’s speed claims. If the result holds up in real use, the price of near-frontier capability just fell again. That matters more to people building agents than a new top model would.

Overstated: “AI grounded America’s flights.” The only link so far is timing, and officials and the contractor point to old telecom hardware. The freeze lawsuit also makes for big headlines, but the odds of a court halting AI research under nuisance law look low. Its real effect may be to put the labs’ own risk warnings on the court record.

Uncertain: whether other companies follow United in blocking agents from buying, and who runs Nvidia when it reports on Tuesday.
