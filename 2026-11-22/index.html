Today’s lead · Agents & security

OpenRouter warns that prompts sent to MiniMax models may have been read days later, even on “zero data retention” routes, after a two-million-prompt bait test

A security researcher hid unique fake secrets in AI requests and watched where they turned up. The answer: in places the customer was never told about, including three breached hosting providers.

On Saturday OpenRouter, a popular service that routes API requests to whichever company hosts a model, emailed customers: “Requests you sent to MiniMax models may have been read.” Test links inside prompts sent to models from MiniMax, a Chinese AI lab, were opened by a third party 40 to 130 hours after the answer, even on requests marked “zero data retention” (ZDR), a label promising nothing is kept.

Where it comes from: HD Moore, chief research officer at the security firm Dragos, published the study on 19 November; wider coverage followed on 20–21 November. He sent about two million prompts to popular AI services, each carrying “canaries”: a never-used web address and a fake Amazon Web Services (AWS) key. Anyone who later opens the link or tries the key sets off an alarm showing where the prompt went.

What Dragos says it found: evidence that attackers had broken into three unnamed hosting providers; at the largest, a couple of hundred bait keys were tried within hours and drove tens of thousands of AWS calls. Resellers on an inference marketplace could read and keep prompts with ordinary request logging, no hacking needed. Requests often passed through undisclosed extra middlemen. And a Chinese AI lab, unnamed, fetched secret links from a large share of the prompts it handled.

OpenRouter’s response: three hosts, AtlasCloud, Novita and StreamLake, told it they passed MiniMax requests on to MiniMax itself. OpenRouter says that broke their data agreements; it has removed those providers, stripped the ZDR label from MiniMax endpoints and says it will replace providers’ self-declared promises with technical checks. It says its own systems were not breached. MiniMax’s side has reached the public only second-hand: per OpenRouter, MiniMax said content moderation was on before 22 October and ZDR is now enabled.

Not established: who the breached providers are, independent confirmation, or that the MiniMax link-openings and the AWS-key abuse are connected.

Why it matters: agents and coding tools routinely paste code, logs and credentials into prompts. This is the first broad evidence that a “we keep nothing” label can fail several hops downstream, out of sight of developer and router. Our reading: the lesson is not “avoid model X” but “assume any prompt may be stored and read.” The mitigation the sources support: keep live secrets out of prompts, filter them automatically, and plant your own canaries.

The Quick Scan

Privacy & agents · 21 November

Signal now blocks screenshots by default on iOS 27 so Siri’s AI can’t read chats, and switches protection on in Android when an assistant looks

Assistants such as Siri and Gemini increasingly read the screen to help. Signal 8.37 on iOS 27 turns on a “Block Siri AI” setting that stops screenshots and recordings (pausable for 10 minutes); on supported Android phones it enables screen security when it detects an assistant reading the screen. Users can opt out. Signal calls the fix incomplete: agents with file access can still reach stored data, and the Mac app is not covered. It wants Apple, Google and Microsoft to offer apps a proper system-level opt-out from AI access.

Research · paper dated 20 November

A day after the Riemann claim, Alpöge and NYU’s Tristan Buckmaster say fluids governed by Navier–Stokes can “blow up” with no outside push

Whether the fluid equations of Navier–Stokes can break down (“blow up”) in finite time is another Millennium Prize problem. OpenAI’s September claim relied on a finely tuned outside force, a loophole in the official wording. Buckmaster and Levent Alpöge (Anthropic) posted a 79-page paper claiming blowup with no force at all, the physically meaningful case, using Claude and Codex throughout. Reports say some files and a key certificate are not yet released and nothing is peer reviewed: a serious, unconfirmed claim.

Scientific American · The Next Web, 21 Nov · background: Quanta, 8 Sep

Policy · rules in force since 19 November

New US export rules reach ordinary phone chips: TSMC and Samsung pause some shipments while they check licences

The US rules aimed at AI chips reach further than expected, per Nikkei Asia and the Financial Times: exemptions for smartphone and other non-AI chips were removed, and foundries must treat chips made at 14/16 nanometres or finer as restricted unless the designer is on a US approved list. TSMC and Samsung have paused some shipments to Chinese customers while they review. A lawyer quoted by the FT expects phone-chip licences to be granted, but delays could disrupt production. Details come from press reports, not the rule text.

Companies · 21 November

Routine database maintenance broke OpenAI’s login service, failing up to 75% of ChatGPT requests for three and a half hours

Per OpenAI’s post-incident write-up, as reported, errors peaked at 75% on ChatGPT between 1:48 and 5:21 Pacific time (10:48–14:21 CET) on Saturday, hitting login, search, uploads, voice and several API endpoints. Scheduled maintenance made both read copies of a database fail at once, and that service handles authentication across OpenAI’s stack. OpenAI says it has paused similar maintenance. Agents built on one provider inherit its single points of failure.

Worth a Look

Creative tools · Google, 19 November

Google’s Genie 4 lets developers stream playable, AI-generated worlds into their own apps

Genie 4 is a “world model”: describe a place and it generates a scene you can walk through in real time, at 720p and 24 frames per second with sound, steered by keys and live text prompts. Since 19 November developers can call it through the Gemini API and Google AI Studio, at a reported $0.10 per second of output, paid tier only.

For interaction designers, the interface is a continuous scene that responds to input, so a game level or museum walkthrough becomes a prompt plus a controller. At $6 a minute, it is a prototyping tool for now.

Signal Check

A privacy label is only as good as the last company in a chain you cannot see.

Deserves attention: the canary study. It is measurement, not theory: bait keys that were actually used. With Signal’s move, it shows AI systems reaching data users never meant to share.

Overstated: “Chinese labs steal your prompts.” The evidence points at one lab and several middlemen; the worst key abuse came via breached providers that are unnamed.

Uncertain: MiniMax’s side of the story, and whether two AI-assisted Millennium Prize claims in two days survive checking. Both need primary documents we do not yet have.
