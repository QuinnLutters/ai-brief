Today’s lead · Chips & policy

Chinese loan filings show Nvidia’s banned Blackwell servers running in a Shanghai data centre tied to Megaspeed

Bloomberg traced the hardware through companies’ own paperwork. It is the clearest evidence yet that America’s top AI chips are working inside China. How they got there is still unknown.

What happened: On Friday Bloomberg reported that two Chinese companies had installed servers built on Nvidia’s Blackwell chips at the “Yangtze River Delta Project”, a data centre in Shanghai. Blackwell is Nvidia’s current flagship generation and cannot legally be exported to China. Bloomberg found the equipment listed in financial documents the companies filed with Chinese authorities; one report says the site also lists 32 servers with older H200 chips.

The Megaspeed link: Megaspeed is the Singapore company that the US added to its trade blacklist this week, as covered in Tuesday’s edition. According to the reports, a second firm at the site provides services to Megaspeed, and Caixin reports that the firm controlling Shanghai Kezhan is run by Megaspeed’s founder, Alice Huang.

Bloomberg says it found no evidence that Megaspeed itself moved the equipment to the data centre. Megaspeed denies wrongdoing; Megaspeed and Nvidia did not respond to Bloomberg.
As reported by Anadolu Agency and The Factum, 27 November

Why it matters: Until now, the case that top Nvidia chips were reaching China rested on trade data, investigators’ suspicions and blacklist notices. This is paperwork from inside China putting named, restricted hardware at a named site. It lands a day after Nvidia paused server shipments to four Southeast Asian countries (yesterday’s lead). Our reading: the important unknown is the route. If the servers passed through a third country despite licence checks, the new rules have a gap. If they were bought before the rules applied, this is an old leak coming to light.

The Quick Scan

Companies & legal · 27 November

OpenAI countersues Apple, accusing it of taking trade secrets through a rehired engineer and of pressuring the hardware suppliers both companies use

The countersuit, filed in federal court in California, answers Apple’s July lawsuit against OpenAI, its device unit io Products and two former Apple staff. OpenAI claims Apple rehired an engineer who had spent about six months at OpenAI and gained confidential information that way. It also says Apple has been “coercing and threatening” shared manufacturers, and that Apple keeps access to former employees’ personal devices and accounts. Apple denies using the engineer that way. The suppliers aren’t named, and the trial in Apple’s case is set for July 2027. What it shows is that OpenAI’s hardware ambitions now put it in direct conflict with the company that controls the iPhone supply chain.

CNBC (FT) · 9to5Mac · Interesting Engineering, 27 Nov

Security · reported 26–27 November

SAP confirms a security incident after reports that attackers used stolen staff tokens to copy and change its code on GitHub

Der Spiegel reported late on Thursday, citing an insider, that attackers stole SAP employees’ authentication tokens (login credentials that grant access without a password) and used them to copy and alter source code in SAP’s GitHub repositories. SAP confirmed it had found suspicious activity and says it has no evidence that customers’ business data was accessed. Handelsblatt reports SAP restricted GitHub access, paused automated build pipelines and replaced its code-signing keys. Spiegel’s source says the attackers may have used AI agents; that is unconfirmed, and most coverage traces back to the same insider. Unknown: how the tokens were taken, which code was touched, and whether altered code reached customers.

Techzine · The Next Web · Security Affairs, 27 Nov

Models & pricing · 27 November

OpenAI says it under-counted GPT-6.2 usage at launch; after the fix, ChatGPT subscribers hit Astra limits about 40% sooner

OpenAI’s Thibault Sottiaux said a billing error since the 17 November launch had been discounting usage of GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol for most users. With it fixed, plans run out roughly 40% faster on Astra and 25% faster on Sol than last week. OpenAI won’t charge for the difference retroactively, says other models weren’t affected, and is giving Go, Plus, Pro and Business subscribers one extra “banked” limit reset. A small story, but a useful reminder: the subscription limits people see are an internal meter that can change without the price changing.

The Decoder · The Next Web · Sulat, 27 Nov

Privacy & impact · auction Monday 1 December

Character.AI’s user chats, including those of about 2.1 million minors, are due at a bankruptcy auction on Monday as dozens of states object

The bankrupt chatbot company plans to auction most of its assets on 1 December, with a $10 million opening bid from Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC. PPC Land reports the bidder was formed a day before it was picked. A bipartisan coalition of state attorneys general argues the sale could hand over chat logs from about 21 million US accounts, along with IP addresses and internal risk scores, to a buyer with no duty to honour Character.AI’s privacy policy or deletion requests. They are asking the Delaware court to keep children’s chats and models trained on them out of the sale. Reports put the coalition at 40 to 43 attorneys general; the court has not ruled. Character.AI says it does not sell personal information.

Signal Check

Today’s stories are about custody: of chips, of code, of chats. The models themselves barely feature.

Deserves attention: the Shanghai filings. Companies’ own regulatory paperwork is harder to dismiss than trade-flow estimates. And Character.AI’s auction on Monday will show whether intimate chat logs count as ordinary company assets when a chatbot company goes under.

Overstated: “AI agents hacked SAP.” One anonymous source said agents may have been used, and repeated coverage does not add confirmation. Likewise, the Apple–OpenAI filings are each side’s case, not findings.

Uncertain: how the Blackwell servers reached Shanghai and when; whether any altered SAP code shipped; and whether the court will strip children’s data from the sale before Monday.
