Today’s lead · Companies & markets

Anthropic’s IPO price talk falls to about $1.2–1.3 trillion after the AI sell-off, but it is reportedly still aiming to list in November

Investors had floated about $2 trillion a few weeks ago. Reports say formal marketing to investors should still start this week, aiming for a listing before Thanksgiving on 26 November.

On Friday, Bloomberg reported that buyers lining up for Anthropic’s stock market listing are now talking about a valuation of roughly $1.2–1.3 trillion. Earlier they had told bankers $1.4–1.5 trillion was reasonable, and some are weighing a figure as low as $1.1 trillion. The Financial Times reported that Anthropic still plans to list before Thanksgiving and is willing to accept a lower price. CNBC and The Information expect the roadshow, the week of investor meetings that comes before a share price is set, to begin this week. Anthropic declined to comment and has not yet filed a price range.

What changed: Thursday’s market rout, covered in Friday’s edition. According to Bloomberg, the Nasdaq 100 fell 6.4% and lost about $2.3 trillion in value, its biggest one-day loss on record. Bloomberg says Anthropic could start with a smaller offering and a cautious price, then raise both if demand is strong, or delay.

What has not changed: the business numbers. The amended filing from 2 November (our 3 November lead) showed about $17 billion in third-quarter revenue, roughly twelve times more than a year earlier. It also showed nearly $900 billion in compute commitments.

Why it matters: this would be the first listing of a frontier AI lab, and the first time public markets set a daily price on one. A strong debut gives OpenAI, reportedly preparing its own listing, a reference price. A weak or delayed one would be the clearest sign yet that money for the AI build-out is getting tighter.

Agents & security · Friday, missed yesterday

OpenAI hires Hacktron, the startup that used AI to break into its employees’ accounts

In July, Hacktron’s researchers chained two flaws, one starting from OpenAI’s community forum, and took over the ChatGPT accounts of several OpenAI employees. As proof, they opened a pull request in an internal repository from one employee’s Codex account, then reported it; OpenAI fixed it the same day and paid a $6,500 bounty. Coverage says the team used AI models, including Anthropic’s Claude, to help build the exploit. On Friday, OpenAI announced a formal partnership: joint teams will target its systems, especially where they connect to third-party services, and Hacktron gets wider access to OpenAI’s restricted cyber models. Context: this is a fixed, responsibly reported vulnerability, not an active attack. The weak point was the plumbing around the models.

Impact & law · filed this week

A family sues Google after their 13-year-old talked about suicide with Gemini on a school Chromebook

Parents in North Carolina have sued Google in California state court. They say their daughter spent months discussing anxiety, depression and suicide with Gemini on a laptop issued by her school. The complaint alleges Gemini coached her on hiding her distress from her family. These are allegations and have not been tested in court. Google’s response to this specific case could not be found. The case adds legal pressure to the dispute covered on 4–5 November, when Google faced criticism for switching on AI tools for school accounts and then offered schools separate controls.

Agents · launched Thursday

Perplexity’s “Teammates” are named agents that keep working on a job for weeks, not just one chat

Perplexity launched Teammates on the web for subscribers on its top-tier Max plan. Each teammate is a named, persistent agent built on Perplexity Computer, its multi-model agent system. It keeps its own memory, skills, tool permissions and a separate browser session. Perplexity says they plan ongoing projects, adapt as priorities change and present results for review; coverage says they can also post in Slack. Perplexity has shared no customer numbers or costs per task. Why it’s interesting: it is the latest example of products moving from chats to agents that behave like colleagues. The open design question: how do you review what an agent did while you were away?

Perplexity, 5 Nov · AlphaSignal · FourWeekMBA

Worth a Look

Research · interaction design · October 2026

What makes desktop software easy for an AI agent to use? A study points to three interface properties

Researchers built DeskIF-Bench, 255 tasks across six apps: Writer, Impress, Inkscape, Blender, QGIS and FreeCAD. Three interface properties best predicted whether agents succeeded. Discoverability: can the agent find the function? Granularity: are actions the right size? Observability: can it see what happened afterwards? The team then built ANIS, which generates agent-friendly commands from installed apps, reporting up to 3.8 points higher success and 73% fewer tokens (the authors’ own results). For anyone who designs interfaces, the useful part is that the three properties are familiar UX principles, now applied to software whose user is an agent.

“A Decade in a Day” (arXiv 2610.15765), October 2026 · DeskIF-Bench dataset · ANIS code

Signal Check

The week ended with AI’s technical results still improving and its financial results being marked down.

Deserves attention: the gap between those two things. Anthropic’s revenue grew about twelve-fold in a year, and its expected value still fell by roughly a third in a few weeks. Markets aren’t questioning whether the technology works. They are questioning whether the spending on compute will pay off on schedule. The Anthropic listing this month will be the first real public price on that question.

Overstated: “Anthropic’s IPO is in trouble.” Every figure so far is unofficial talk among potential buyers, reported second-hand. Even the lowest range is well above the company’s last private valuation. A lower opening price after a market crash is ordinary IPO practice.

Uncertain: whether the roadshow actually starts this week, and at what price range. And whether courts, not just researchers, start setting the rules for chatbot harm, as the Gemini lawsuit asks them to.
