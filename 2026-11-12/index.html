Today’s lead · Companies & markets

OpenAI made $10 billion last quarter and lost $10.9 billion, according to documents seen by the Wall Street Journal

Revenue grew about 50% in three months and business customers are now its biggest source of income. But it still spends more than a dollar for every dollar it earns, a week before Anthropic, which publishes its numbers, goes public.

On Wednesday morning the Wall Street Journal reported figures from internal OpenAI documents for July to September. Revenue reached about $10 billion, up roughly 50% from the previous quarter. That is the company’s first $10 billion quarter. Over the same period its adjusted operating loss grew from $10.1 billion to about $10.9 billion. (“Adjusted” means some costs, such as stock pay, are left out.)

Reported: CFO Sarah Friar said enterprise revenue doubled in the quarter and now matches consumer revenue, and that the Microsoft Azure contract had held sales back. Not confirmed: OpenAI has not commented or published a cost breakdown.

The comparison everyone is making: Anthropic’s amended IPO filing shows $16.96 billion in revenue for the same quarter and an operating loss of $567 million. The comparison is rough: one is an SEC filing, the other adjusted figures from investor documents. But the gap is large.

Why it matters: OpenAI’s losses per dollar fell by about a quarter in three months, yet it still burns roughly $11 billion a quarter while preparing its own listing. Once Anthropic trades next week, OpenAI will be measured against a rival whose accounts are public.

The Quick Scan

Models & APIs

Google’s Gemini Decision 1 answers up to 1,000 questions at once with probabilities instead of text, for $0.10 per million input tokens

Most AI models answer by writing text. Google’s new Gemini Decisions API skips that step. You send it some material (text, images, audio, video or PDFs) and a list of yes/no questions, multiple-choice questions or scoring rubrics, and it returns an answer to each with a probability attached. With no text written, output is free. Google’s claims: it is calibrated, meaning a 70% answer should be right about 70% of the time, and it beats rivals on five of seven benchmarks. No independent tests yet. Why it’s interesting: a large share of what developers ask models to do is really classification: checking content, sorting tickets or grading agent output. Honest confidence scores would make that cheaper and easier to audit.

Agents & security

NEAR AI says attackers got in through a flaw in a popular open-source AI server and reached 18 agents and about 100 user accounts

NEAR AI, which hosts AI agents, said on 9 November that an intrusion began the day before through a remote code execution flaw in SGLang, a widely used open-source engine for running models. According to the company, the attackers reached about 100 user accounts and 18 older agents that ran outside its protected “confidential computing” environment. Chat histories, settings and logs were exposed; two customers’ API keys found in logs were revoked. Status: confirmed breach, per the company. NEAR did not say which SGLang vulnerability was used, and SGLang has had several this year, some still unpatched. What the sources support: anyone running SGLang should update to the latest version and keep its control endpoints off the open internet.

Worth a Look

Research · announced 10 November

DeepMind’s AI agents proved that Signal’s encryption works as designed, checking the actual compiled code rather than a description of it

Mathematically proving that code does what it should is called formal verification, and it normally takes expert teams years. Google DeepMind says its multi-agent system, AlphaProof Code, did it in four weeks. The system checked the key exchange (PQXDH, which is designed to resist future quantum computers) and the “Triple Ratchet”, which keeps changing the keys during a conversation. It did not check the source code but the compiled 64-bit Arm machine code of libsignal 0.105.4, the instructions a phone actually runs, closing the gap where a compiler could slip in a flaw. The proofs are written in Lean, a language in which a computer checks every step. Older versions with known, fixed bugs failed to verify, as they should. Limits: this is not the whole Signal app. Timing and power side-channel attacks were out of scope, and no new bugs were found. Reports of the proof’s size range from 3 million to 6.5 million lines. DeepMind has published the proofs on GitHub for anyone to check.

Signal Check

The same week AI showed it can prove software is correct, an AI company was breached through the software that runs its models.

Deserves attention: the AlphaProof Code result. Checking compiled machine code, with proofs anyone can re-run, is a sturdier kind of claim than a benchmark score. The NEAR breach is the other side. Agent platforms rest on young open-source model servers, and these are now a real way in.

Overstated: “Claude Sheets sank Microsoft”. A 2% drop on a day of hot inflation figures, caused by a product its own maker calls basic, is not a verdict on Excel. The OpenAI–Anthropic comparison also deserves care: adjusted figures from leaked documents are not the same as a filing with the SEC.

Uncertain: whether Gemini Decision’s confidence scores hold up in independent tests. That calibration is the main reason to use it. Also uncertain: whether OpenAI really cuts Cursor off today. 12 November was its proposed date, and nothing confirmed it by this morning.
