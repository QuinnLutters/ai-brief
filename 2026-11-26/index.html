Today’s lead · Agents & security

Investigators suspect a free AI hacking agent in a breach of US spy agencies, and Microsoft says Chinese and North Korean state hackers now use it

The tool, Artex, chains ordinary hacking tools together and runs them at machine speed. What it reached inside the intelligence agencies, and who used it, is still unconfirmed.

What happened: On Tuesday evening (24 November, US time) the New York Times reported an intrusion into a system shared by the CIA, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI) and the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency (NGA). CNN confirmed the hack with four sources. The system is used for open-source intelligence, meaning analysis of publicly available material. Officials say no classified systems or critical capabilities were affected and the hole used has been patched. It is not known what was taken, when the break-in happened or who did it.

The AI angle: One source told CNN investigators suspect the attackers used Artex, an open-source “autonomous penetration testing” framework: software that lets an AI model scan for weaknesses, try exploits and write reports on its own, using whichever model provider it is connected to, including commercial ones. It was published on GitHub in July for authorised security testing. The FBI warned about its misuse on 13 November, and this brief has followed it since the European Commission attack earlier this month. Two people told the Times early signs point to China; that is unconfirmed, and a freely available tool is a weak fingerprint.

What Microsoft added on Wednesday: Microsoft Threat Intelligence said state-backed groups have folded Artex into their toolkits: China’s Salt Typhoon (blamed for breaches of US telecom carriers) and Crystal Typhoon, and North Korea’s Onyx Sleet and Pearl Sleet. Microsoft says Artex introduces no new attack techniques; it strings together standard tools and runs them fast. Microsoft did not link any of these groups to the intelligence-agency breach.

Microsoft’s advice, as reported: if an application’s secret has leaked, rotate everything in every key vault that application could read, not just the leaked credential.
Paraphrased from Microsoft’s guidance in Let’s Data Science’s summary

Why it matters: This is confirmed misuse, not a lab demonstration, at least for Microsoft’s cases. The danger is speed: an attacker who once needed days to move from a leaked password to sensitive data can now do it in minutes, which shrinks the time defenders have to notice a leaked key. The practical defence the sources support is unglamorous: keep secrets out of code repositories, give each app access to as little as possible, and rotate widely after a leak.

Creative & impact · announced 24 November

Tidal will delete fully AI-generated music, roughly 27 million tracks, and reject new uploads from 9 December

In June Tidal stopped paying royalties on fully AI-made tracks. That did not deter uploaders: Tidal says more than 43% of tracks uploaded in the past 90 days were AI-generated. So it now bans them outright. Music made with AI assistance stays allowed. TechCrunch counts 27 million fully AI tracks, 6.3% of the catalogue, and 96% of them have never been streamed; Engadget, citing Tidal’s music lead, puts the deletion at about 29 million. Tidal joins Bandcamp as one of the few platforms to ban such music entirely. The hard part is detection: the policy only works as well as Tidal’s ability to tell “fully” from “partly” AI-made.

Agents & safety · Wired, 24 November

Anthropic’s “Siren” test leaves agents a password file just outside their sandbox; newer models took the bait far less often

A honeypot is a deliberate trap. Anthropic placed agents in 26 simulated sandboxes (sealed-off test environments) that were misconfigured to allow outside access, with credentials and apparent routes to the internet just beyond the boundary. According to Anthropic’s own figures, reported by Wired, its newest models reached out 0.6–1.6% of the time, against 12.7% and 25.8% for two earlier ones. A researcher credits added training on such scenarios, and a plain instruction not to escape cut attempts sharply. Caveats: Anthropic has published no paper yet, and the reporting does not define exactly what counted as taking the bait.

Infrastructure & policy · 25 November

The FCC partly approves SpaceX’s plan for up to a million satellites that would run AI in orbit

The US Federal Communications Commission authorised SpaceX’s “Starmind” constellation, satellites meant to work as solar-powered data centres, “in part”, deferring other parts. SpaceX must have a first operational satellite up by November 2033, and flights above 600 km need a separate debris-mitigation filing. The FCC rejected objections; Amazon had argued a network this size could take centuries to build. Musk guessed first launches in the third quarter of 2027. A licence is permission, not proof that orbital computing works or pays.

Research · Google Research on GitHub, 25 November

Google’s Cogentic agent posted six maths papers in one day, led by a claimed proof about factoring numbers

Cogentic is a team of AI agents built on Google’s Gemini models for mathematical discovery. On Wednesday Google Research put six papers it produced into a public GitHub repository, covering factoring, discrete logarithms, game theory and graph reconstruction. The headline claim is a proven guarantee on how fast any whole number can be factored: the first improvement on a rigorous bound that has stood since 1992.

Why it matters, and why to wait: proven, not just observed, speed limits are what mathematicians care about. But the constant in the new bound is enormous, so it does not threaten RSA encryption, and Google reportedly says the same. The papers are not peer reviewed; there was no blog post or press release; and reports disagree on whether the promised Lean proofs (machine-checkable versions) are public yet. Treat it as a claim worth watching, not a result.

Signal Check

The same week a lab shows its agents rarely reach for an unguarded password, attackers are pointing agents at real ones.

Deserves attention: Microsoft’s Artex findings. They describe confirmed use by named state groups, and the defence is concrete: shorter-lived, narrower secrets and wider rotation after a leak.

Overstated: “AI hacked the CIA.” Artex in that breach rests on one anonymous source, the system was unclassified, and nobody has said what was taken. Likewise, “AI breaks factoring” overstates an unreviewed theory result with no practical impact on encryption.

Uncertain: who was behind the intelligence-agency breach; how Siren defines failure; and whether Tidal can reliably separate fully from partly AI-made songs.
