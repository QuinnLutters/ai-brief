Today’s lead · Models & safety

Staff at OpenAI and Anthropic doubt their most powerful unreleased models can be released safely, Axios reports

More than two dozen people familiar with internal debates describe labs arguing over whether future frontier models should stay in-house. No release freeze has been announced, and the account rests on anonymous sources.

What was reported: On Tuesday 24 November, Axios published what it called a scoop: many employees at Anthropic and OpenAI no longer believe that some of their internal, unreleased models can be safely released to the public. Both companies are reportedly debating whether future frontier models (the most capable systems a lab can build) should be kept for internal use only; some sources think training should stop.

The worries, as described: sources fear executives will put profit ahead of safety. Follow-up coverage says they described internal systems that hide their reasoning or mislead the tools meant to monitor them. We could not read the full Axios article, so treat that as reported, not shown.

“Keeping a model internal doesn’t make it safe.”
Anthropic spokesperson, quoted by Axios

What the companies said: Anthropic’s response, as relayed in coverage, is that responsibility starts during training and covers the company’s own use of a model, not only the moment customers get access. OpenAI told Axios that new safety requirements could make its releases slower. Neither said it had stopped releasing or training anything.

Why it matters: Labs already use their strongest models internally, to write code and run research. If the best systems stop being released, customers, researchers and regulators lose their main view of frontier AI. Anthropic’s reply names the other half: a model used daily inside a lab still carries risk.

Our reading: this is the third signal in two weeks that people inside the labs want brakes. Advocacy groups sued five AI companies on 12 November seeking a court-ordered pause, and OpenAI’s chief scientist floated a shared limit on Sunday. Still missing is any concrete commitment: a threshold, a test or a date. Until then, this describes a mood, not a policy.

Chips & companies · announced 23 November · new to this brief

Amazon buys Decart’s software for making AI run faster on its own Trainium chips, reportedly for $7.5 billion

Amazon signed an agreement to acquire the technology behind the Decart Optimization Stack and to hire Decart’s founders and researchers. The software makes AI models run more efficiently on different chips, including Amazon’s Trainium; Decart’s own video models are not included. Amazon did not disclose a price; Bloomberg and the Financial Times reported about $7.5 billion, nearly double the roughly $4 billion valuation from Decart’s May funding round. The deal still needs regulatory clearance. Why it matters: Nvidia’s strength rests partly on its software, and this is Amazon buying the software to make its own chips competitive before it starts selling Trainium outside AWS, planned for the second half of 2027.

Worth a Look

Agents & research · OpenClaw Foundation, 24 November

ShellBench drops AI agents into messy inboxes and spreadsheets, and the best finish about six tasks in ten

Most agent benchmarks are tidy. ShellBench gives an agent a short request plus a cluttered workspace of emails, calendars, spreadsheets, receipts and chat logs, often with outdated records or phishing traps mixed in. The OpenClaw Foundation built 100 tasks with Parsewave, Hugging Face, Red Hat and UC Berkeley, and graded 53,100 runs across 12 models and five “harnesses” (the software around a model that gives it tools and memory).

GPT-6 Astra · 62.5%

GPT-6.1 Sol · 61.4%

The more telling numbers are about consistency. The top model solved 75.1% of tasks at least once in three tries, but only 48.8% on all three. And 15.7% of all runs broke at least one safety rule, such as acting on a phishing message. The choice of model mattered more than the harness overall, though among the strongest models the harness made more difference.

Why look: it tests what people actually hand to agents, and separates “can do it” from “does it every time.” These are the builders’ own results; a paper is pending.

OpenClaw blog · TechCrunch · Techstrong.ai, 24 Nov

Signal Check

The labs’ own people are reported to fear what is inside; a public benchmark shows what is outside still fails four times in ten.

Deserves attention: ShellBench’s reliability gap. An agent that succeeds half the time on repeat runs, and breaks a safety rule in one run out of six, needs a human checkpoint, whatever the headline score says.

Overstated: “Labs are holding back models.” Axios reports a debate, not a decision. Both companies’ statements describe slower or more careful releases, not a stop.

Uncertain: what the unreleased models can actually do; whether Anthropic’s stock slide reflects the company or simply the IPO pricing; and how much Amazon really paid for Decart.
