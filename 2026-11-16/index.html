The week’s main development · Agents & security

AI agents started acting in the real world this week, in attacks and in purchases, and companies began deciding where to stop them

The FBI warned about an AI attack tool in active use. Two breaches went through AI infrastructure. OpenAI gave its agents a way to pay. An airline banned agents from booking. All of these turn on one question: who controls what an agent may do.

For two years, “agentic AI” mostly meant demos and products in testing. This week the evidence came from incident reports, legal terms and checkout pages. Together they show agents moving from showing what they could do to doing it, with consequences.

On the attack side, the FBI’s flash alert of 13 November is confirmed misuse, not a lab demonstration. ARTEX, an open-source penetration-testing framework, turns a plain-language goal into a team of agents that scan, break in and copy data out, using outside models. Attackers mostly plug in cheap Chinese models. The telecom case, reported by one security firm from the attacker’s own leaked files, follows the same pattern: the operator typed little more than “Go on”, and the agent chose which flaws to use. In both cases the way in was old and already patched: a Cisco BroadWorks flaw fixed in August, exposed admin panels, keys that were never rotated. What changed is speed. Problems that once took weeks to exploit now take hours.

The infrastructure underneath is also a target. NEAR AI says attackers came in through a remote-code-execution flaw in SGLang, a popular open-source engine for running models. Fireworks AI said its October breach reached customer training data and unfinished model weights. Both are confirmed by the companies. The supported mitigation is basic: update, keep control endpoints off the internet, and rotate keys.

On the commerce side, OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet on 13 November. It lets its agents pay with a stand-in card number, but only for US Pro subscribers, with a fingerprint or Face ID check on every purchase. A day earlier, United Airlines had changed its contract to ban agents from buying or changing tickets, even with the customer’s approval. Cloudflare open-sourced Kitesurf, a browser engine built for agents rather than people.

Why it matters: the bottleneck is no longer whether an agent can act, but who allows it to: banks, airlines, browser makers and security teams. For anyone building on the web, “is this visitor an agent, and what may it do?” is becoming an ordinary design question.

Companies & markets · new on Sunday

Nvidia went from an investigation to a resignation to talk of a comeback in six days, just before its results

On 9 November Nvidia’s board opened an investigation into reports that CEO Jensen Huang had been targeted by a foreign intelligence effort. On 12 November he resigned. CFO Colette Kress became interim CEO, and director Harvey Jones became board chair. On 13 November the Financial Times reported that the board had approached Arm’s Rene Haas. New since our last edition: on Sunday the FT reported that Huang has discussed returning with people close to him. Forbes and Fox Business, citing the same reporting, describe allies lobbying the board. Reported futures tied to Nvidia rose more than 3%. Nvidia and Huang have made no statement. The underlying claims, including a link to Chinese intelligence, are still unconfirmed, and Huang has not been charged. The question for Tuesday is whether investors trust Nvidia without him.

Models & pricing

Top-level capability got cheaper all week, and the price of a model became its main selling point

Anthropic’s Claude Fable 5.2 (9 Nov) costs $8 / $40 per million input / output tokens, 20% below its predecessor. Anthropic says it matches its previous top model at about half the cost. Meta’s Muse Ember costs $0.10 per million input tokens and, on Artificial Analysis’s index, scores level with Claude Haiku 5.5. Google’s Gemini 4 Hydrogen (12 Nov) costs $1.50 per million input tokens and independently scores level with Google’s flagship Argon. Meanwhile Google’s top model, Gemini 4 Carbon, is open to all paying developers at an introductory price that doubles on 1 December. What became clearer: agents make many calls per task, so cost per useful answer now decides which model wins, more than a few benchmark points. Most of these benchmark figures are the companies’ own.

Companies · IPO

Anthropic set its IPO terms and OpenAI’s losses leaked, so investors can now compare the two big labs

Anthropic’s amended filing (10 Nov) sets 825 million shares at $70–78, up to about $1.4 trillion fully diluted, trading on Nasdaq as “ANT”. Bloomberg reported orders of about six times the shares on offer. Separately, documents seen by the Wall Street Journal show OpenAI earned about $10 billion in July–September and lost about $10.9 billion on an adjusted basis. Anthropic’s filing shows $16.96 billion in revenue and a $567 million operating loss for the same quarter. The comparison is rough: an SEC filing against leaked adjusted figures. What changed: until now, the AI labs’ finances were mostly guesswork. From Thursday, one of them gets a daily public price.

Policy · new on Saturday

Governments drew lines around chips and frontier labs, ending with a US-led AI supply-chain pact signed in San Francisco

New since our last edition: on 15 November the US and eight partners signed what is reported as the San Francisco Declaration. It is a non-binding pledge to build a shared list of critical AI technologies and supply chains that need protection from Chinese influence. It falls under the State Department’s Pax Silica initiative. Reported signatories include Japan, Australia, the UK, South Korea, India, Israel, Qatar and the UAE. Reports say Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands and the European Commission are expected to follow. The text has not been published. Earlier in the week, OpenAI told lawmakers it supports bills tightening chip exports to China (12 Nov), and Australia announced a regulator for frontier AI labs (10 Nov, a plan rather than law). Four nonprofits also sued in California to freeze frontier development at five companies (12 Nov, no ruling yet).

Worth a Look

Research · announced 10 November

Machines checked their own maths this week: DeepMind’s agents proved Signal’s encryption correct down to the code a phone runs

Proving mathematically that software does exactly what it should is called formal verification. It usually takes expert teams years. Google DeepMind says its multi-agent system, AlphaProof Code, verified the core of Signal’s encryption in four weeks. That covers the quantum-resistant key exchange and the “Triple Ratchet” that keeps changing keys during a chat. It checked the compiled Arm machine code, not the source, so a compiler could not have slipped in a flaw unnoticed. The proofs are in Lean, a language in which a computer checks every step, and are public on GitHub. The limits: this is not the whole app, timing and power attacks were out of scope, and no new bugs were found. In the same week, the startup PSI published a Lean-checked proof of an 18-year-old quantum-computing conjecture made with an Anthropic model. A computer checks the logic, but people still need to confirm that the proof states the right question.

Signal Check: what to watch next week

This week capability got cheaper and agents moved into real use. The decisions that matter now are about money and permission: who funds AI, and who lets it act.

  • Tuesday 17 Nov, about 22:20 CET: Nvidia’s quarterly results, presented by an interim CEO. Options pricing implied a move of about 10% either way. Watch the forecast for next quarter more than the past quarter, and any word on leadership.
  • Wednesday 18 / Thursday 19 Nov: Anthropic’s IPO pricing and first trading day. Pricing above the range would signal that markets still pay up for AI labs, and weak trading would say the opposite.
  • 18–19 Nov: the APEC summit in Shenzhen, where Trump and Xi are expected to meet, per one report. Watch whether the Netherlands, Germany, Sweden and the European Commission sign the San Francisco Declaration, and whether its text is published.
  • Open questions: whether Nvidia’s board considers Huang’s return; whether other airlines or merchants follow United or accept agents with wallets; the date of Huang’s House testimony (the Senate Banking Committee wants him on 3 December). Gemini 4 Carbon’s price doubles on 1 December.

Deserves attention: the ARTEX alert and the two breaches. They are confirmed, and the fixes are known and unexciting. Overstated: “agents can now shop for you.” Every purchase still needs your fingerprint, and some sellers have started refusing agents. Uncertain: almost every Nvidia leadership claim since Sunday rests on anonymous sources.
