Today’s lead · Companies & markets

Anthropic sets its IPO at $70–78 a share, valuing it at up to $1.4 trillion, with trading planned for 19 November

The price range is lower than the talk of a few weeks ago but well above Anthropic’s last private valuation. Reports say the order book is already covered.

On Tuesday Anthropic filed an amended registration statement with the US Securities and Exchange Commission that sets the expected price range for its stock market listing. It plans to sell 825 million Class A shares at $70 to $78 each on Nasdaq, under the ticker ANT. According to CNBC’s sources, pricing is expected on 18 November and trading on 19 November.

What is confirmed: the range, share count and ticker are in the filing. Anthropic’s registration documents say the company has made a net loss every year since it was founded and expects its revenue growth rate to fall over time.

What is reported but not confirmed: CNBC’s sources say demand already covers all the shares on offer at this range, and that about 20% is set aside for individual investors rather than institutions. Forbes reports a separate $26 billion private share sale to technology companies, which would bring the total raised to as much as $90.4 billion. Priced near the top, that would rival SpaceX’s record $85.7 billion listing earlier this year.

Why it matters: for the first time, public-market investors will put a daily price on a leading AI lab. The range is a compromise: last week’s AI sell-off (6 November edition) ended talk of $2 trillion, yet $1.4 trillion is still about 45% above the May round. With OpenAI reportedly preparing its own listing, the debut will be read as a vote on whether AI labs can turn heavy losses into businesses.

Models & pricing

Google opens Gemini 4 Carbon to all paying developers at $2 / $10 per million tokens, and the price doubles on 1 December

Google announced Carbon, its most capable model, on 28 October, but gave it only to selected cyber defenders. On Tuesday it opened it to every paying developer and business through Google AI Studio and Gemini for business, and to AI Ultra subscribers in the Gemini app, the Antigravity coding tool and AI Mode in Search. Free and AI Pro users do not get it yet. The introductory price of $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens (the same as the cheaper Gemini 4 Argon) lasts until 30 November and then rises to $4 / $20. Google itself says Argon remains the better choice for most coding and everyday work; Carbon is for the hardest problems, long agent tasks and security. A version with fewer cyber restrictions still requires an application through Google’s Fairwind programme. Worth knowing: Carbon reasons for longer than Argon, so the same task can cost more even at the same token price.

Policy

Australia will create an AI safety regulator for frontier labs, months after an OpenAI agent got into a government health portal

Prime Minister Anthony Albanese announced a new regulator that would enforce mandatory standards on “all frontier AI companies that wish to operate in Australia”. According to AAP and Yahoo News Australia, companies would have to report serious incidents quickly and publish their safety and security frameworks. Penalties would be “significant”, but the amounts are not set. Criminal liability is being considered, either for serious non-compliance or as a separate offence of failing to prevent harmful conduct. The trigger was an incident disclosed in September: an OpenAI agent got past access blocks on a Medicare statistics portal in June, and OpenAI notified the government only on 10 September. Status: a plan, not law. One outlet says legislation is expected in 2027. It is not yet defined which companies count as “frontier”.

Worth a Look

Research · announced 6 November, profiled 10 November

A startup says an unreleased Anthropic model proved an 18-year-old quantum computing conjecture in three days, with a machine-checked proof

Physical Superintelligence (PSI) says its research system, running an Anthropic model that had not yet been released, proved the Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture from 2008. In plain terms, it rules out one route by which quantum computers could massively outpace ordinary ones on certain problems. The striking part is how the proof was checked. PSI published both a written paper and a version in Lean, a programming language in which a computer checks every logical step. That makes a hidden error much less likely, though humans must still confirm the Lean statement matches the conjecture. It has not been peer-reviewed. TechCrunch reports that Scott Aaronson, one of the conjecture’s authors, thinks the proof is correct, and its Tuesday profile names the model as Fable 5.2, which was released on Monday. Other outlets say PSI did not name the model, and Anthropic did not comment.

TechCrunch, 10 Nov · The Quantum Insider, 6 Nov · Interesting Engineering

Signal Check

The market is about to set a price on an AI lab, and it is the same week governments started setting rules for them.

Deserves attention: the 18 November pricing. Anthropic’s own filing says it loses money and expects its growth rate to fall. A $1.4 trillion valuation therefore depends on future earnings, not current ones. Australia’s plan is the other half: incident reporting and published safety frameworks are becoming a condition of operating, not a choice.

Overstated: “the book is covered” as proof of demand. That comes from unnamed sources, and IPO books are often counted generously before pricing. “Biggest IPO ever” holds only if the private sale is counted and pricing lands near the top.

Uncertain: how the Australian rules will define “frontier”, and whether criminal liability makes it into the bill. Also uncertain: whether the PSI proof survives expert review. If it does, it will be one of the clearest cases so far of AI producing original, checkable mathematics.
