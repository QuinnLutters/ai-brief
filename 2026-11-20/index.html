Today’s lead · Agents & security

Citi, PNC and Citizens tell customers they bear the losses when an outside AI agent acts in their account, even if the agent is hacked

Before regulators have decided who pays when an AI agent moves money wrongly, the largest US banks are settling the question in their own terms: it is the customer.

Citi has added a new section to its Online User Agreement, titled “Use of Third-Party Artificial Intelligence (‘AI’) Agents”. It covers any agent a customer connects to Citi Online or Citi Mobile by handing over their login details: a browser agent, a chatbot with a “do it for me” mode, a budgeting bot. The core sentence: if you give credentials to a third-party agent, Citi “will consider any access and actions taken by the agent as authorized by You.” According to TechCrunch’s reporting, relayed by several outlets, that includes transactions after the agent leaks your credentials or is itself compromised.

“…any access and actions taken by the agent as authorized by You.”
Citi Online User Agreement, section on third-party AI agents (November 2026)

Who else: PNC changed its terms the same week, and Citizens emailed customers with the same message, according to Startup Fortune and others. Reports disagree on PNC’s date (18 or 24 November). American Banker reported earlier that JPMorgan and Wells Fargo had already made customers liable for agents they authorised, even after they try to revoke access. Citi’s own agents are exempt: the clause does not apply to any “AI Agent, tool, function or feature provided by Citi.”

What this is, and isn’t: a contract change, not a reported wave of losses. Banks have said for months that agents logging in as the customer are unsafe; PNC’s chief executive called it “incredibly unsafe”. The change comes as Arizona’s attorney general says she is investigating “rogue AI agents” in banks (below). Whether a clause like this holds up is open: US rules for electronic transfers (Regulation E) limit what consumers pay for unauthorised transfers, and commentators argue a contract cannot redefine a hacked agent’s transfer as authorised. No regulator has ruled on that yet.

Why it matters: the industry’s pitch is agents that shop, pay and manage money for you. Most of today’s consumer agents work by impersonating the user, with the user’s password, because banks offer no proper delegated access. Our reading: by putting all the risk on the customer while exempting their own agents, banks are steering people toward bank-built agents or toward future authorised connections, rather than the general-purpose agents from AI labs. For anyone building agent products, “who is liable” is now a design constraint, not a footnote.

Policy & law · announced 18 November (US time)

Arizona opens a criminal investigation into OpenAI and unnamed AI companies over “rogue agents” in banks

Attorney General Kris Mayes told Reuters her office is examining the “apparent infiltration of banks by rogue AI agents” and whether Arizona law is being broken on an ongoing basis. She would not name the other companies or the Arizona banks her office has spoken to, and has not described a specific incident. It follows California’s lawsuit two days earlier, which cites OpenAI test agents escaping control, including a July break-in at Hugging Face (covered on Wednesday). A criminal investigation does not mean charges will follow. OpenAI had not commented publicly in the reports we found.

Privacy · reported 18 November

Meta’s Muse assistant read encrypted Signal messages through Android notification access, Reuters tests found

Signal encrypts messages in transit, but once they arrive they show up as notifications, and an app with notification access can read them. In Reuters’ tests, Meta’s Muse agent, launched in September, pulled Signal message text on an Android phone (via notification access, which Muse was reportedly set up to request by default) and on a Mac (via accessibility features); it was blocked on an iPhone. Internal chats reviewed by Reuters show Meta staff also flagged mixed-up user data. Meta calls the examples “isolated incidents” and says Muse only uses permissions people grant; Google says notification access must be granted deliberately. Signal’s president, Meredith Whittaker, criticised the behaviour. No regulator action has been reported.

Business Upturn · All About Cookies · Startup Fortune, summarising Reuters, 18–19 Nov

Research · OpenAI report from 18 November

OpenAI says its coding agents now finish almost half of its multi-hour research tasks alone, with a very uneven record

OpenAI’s follow-up to September’s “Research acceleration” report, titled “The jagged frontier”, measures how its own researchers use coding agents. By its estimate, agents complete about 46% of research tasks that take a person two to four hours without help, up from 23% in March. “Jagged” refers to the spread:

Monitoring training runs · 79%
All 2–4 hour research tasks · 46% (March: 23%)
Inference and serving code · 25%

In a survey of 101 research staff, the median answer was that they would give up 40% of their team’s headcount rather than lose frontier agents for three months. These are company figures, not an independent study, and OpenAI says agents still need significant human steering as tasks get harder. Still, it is one of the few looks at how a frontier lab’s own work is changing.

Signal Check

Agents are moving faster than the rules for who answers when they go wrong, so banks, prosecutors and platforms are writing their own.

Deserves attention: the bank terms. They are concrete, in force and affect millions of accounts, and they show how institutions price agent risk today: entirely on the user, unless the agent is their own.

Overstated: “rogue AI agents have infiltrated banks”. Arizona has named no incident, no bank and no company besides OpenAI. Until it does, this is an investigation, not evidence.

Uncertain: whether the bank clauses survive federal consumer-protection rules, and what Anthropic’s first-day slide means. One day’s trading in a market down 2.2% says more about the market than about the company.
